PROBLEM TO SOLVE

Module 1 currently teaches six necessary terms one at a time and includes teaching feedback, but the experience remains definition-heavy. Students can learn ethos as a vocabulary fact without learning how to use it to explain why a communicator’s choice may affect a particular audience and help accomplish a purpose. The saved prompt interpretation is not sufficiently reused while students learn the concepts needed to answer that prompt.

The representative ethos slice must teach transfer, not memorization. Students should leave able to say:

“The communicator made this credibility choice. It may make this audience more willing to trust or listen. That response helps the communicator accomplish this purpose.”

TARGET STUDENT EXPERIENCE

For the gated ethos term:

1. Start with a familiar, age-appropriate communication situation.
2. Ask the student to notice the choice before naming the term.
3. Name ethos and give a concise definition after the student has something concrete to attach it to.
4. Contrast one example with one plausible nonexample.
5. Ask what effect the credibility choice may have on that specific audience.
6. Ask how that audience effect helps the communicator’s purpose.
7. Apply the same three-part lens to a short, inspectable King passage.
8. Ask for one small decision or explanation—not a paragraph and not a generic definition recall.
9. Show how this thinking helps answer the saved assignment prompt.

Each stage has one visible job and one primary action. The student should never face the entire nine-part lesson as one long page.

FAMILIAR EXAMPLE

Choose one concise scenario appropriate for a high-school student—such as deciding whose safety advice to trust, a coach explaining a training plan, or another everyday credibility decision. The scenario must:

- make the communication choice visible;
- identify a real audience;
- support a reasonable purpose;
- avoid requiring specialized background knowledge;
- allow a plausible nonexample;
- avoid stereotypes and culturally narrow assumptions;
- fit on a mobile screen without becoming a reading passage.

Ask the student to notice first. Do not put “This is ethos” in the heading before the noticing decision.

EXAMPLE / NONEXAMPLE

Teach the boundary of the concept:

- Example: a credibility/trust choice that gives the audience a reason to see the communicator as reliable or worth listening to.
- Nonexample: a choice aimed primarily at emotion, evidence/reasoning, decoration, or an unsupported claim of authority.

The student should explain or choose why the example builds credibility. Feedback must teach the distinction rather than say only correct/incorrect.

AUDIENCE EFFECT AND PURPOSE

Make the analytical anchor the center of the lesson:

1. What credibility choice did the communicator make?
2. What might that choice make this audience think or do?
3. How could that response help the communicator accomplish the purpose?

Persist the student’s small decision/explanation without requiring polished prose. Do not score speculative but reasonable audience-effect language as wrong merely because it differs from one model answer. Use bounded choices plus an optional short explanation when needed.

KING APPLICATION

Use one short passage from the saved/authoritative course source whose wording can be verified. The passage must be inspectable in context and must contain a defensible ethos move.

The application should ask the student to identify:

- the credibility choice;
- the intended/relevant audience;
- a likely audience effect;
- how the effect supports King’s purpose.

Keep the task small. A suitable interaction might be one choice followed by one sentence stem, but it must require application rather than copying the definition. Link or disclose enough source context to prevent quote detachment.

Do not fabricate quotation text. If the repository lacks an authoritative passage, stop and report the source gap instead of inventing one.

- Do not overwrite or “improve” the student’s paraphrase automatically.
- Keep it on the shelf during familiar example/definition stages.

The carry-forward helper should be reusable in later Modules 2–4, but this prompt only wires the representative Module 1 slice and adds compatibility assertions. Do not redesign later modules.
