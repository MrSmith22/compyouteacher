# The Writing Learning Engine V2

## Philosophy

The Writing Learning Engine V2 is designed as a **thinking coach**, not an essay writer. Its purpose is to help students learn how writers think, how arguments are built, and how evidence becomes writing. The system exists to support intellectual work that belongs to the student.

Students construct knowledge. They do not move through the assignment by filling in blanks or repeating supplied ideas. They examine content, notice important details, test interpretations, gather support, and gradually build an argument that is genuinely their own.

AI plays a supporting role in that process. AI scaffolds thinking by slowing students down at the right moments, prompting them to return to evidence, asking them to clarify what they mean, and helping them recognize when an idea is still underdeveloped. AI does not supply the student's interpretation, claim, paragraph, or essay.

In this model, evidence comes before claims. Students first build familiarity with the assignment, the content, and the sources. They collect observations before they are asked to make larger arguments. When students do begin forming claims, those claims are expected to remain provisional until the evidence is strong enough to support them.

Claims are strengthened by gathering additional evidence. Instead of treating early claims as final, the system treats them as ideas under construction. Students may discover that an early claim is too broad, too thin, or too weakly supported. That is not failure. It is part of the writing process. The engine should normalize returning to the sources to deepen support and sharpen thinking.

Writing is the final product of thinking, not the starting point. The finished essay matters, but it is the visible result of a longer chain of understanding, noticing, evidence collection, interpretation, and organization. The engine should therefore teach writing by teaching the thinking that makes good writing possible.

### Assignment Understanding

Students first need to understand what kind of thinking the assignment demands. They should know the task, the purpose of the task, the kind of writing they are being asked to produce, and the major question the assignment is asking them to answer.

At this stage, the goal is not to begin writing. The goal is to orient the student so that later evidence collection and idea development are purposeful. Students should leave this stage knowing what they are trying to investigate.

### Content Knowledge

Before students can make meaningful claims, they need enough background knowledge to understand the subject they are writing about. This stage helps students build context, vocabulary, and conceptual familiarity with the topic, text set, issue, event, or problem.

The goal here is to reduce confusion and increase readiness. Students should not be asked to argue about material they barely understand. Content knowledge gives them a base for more careful reading and stronger interpretation.

### Source Understanding

Once students understand the assignment and topic, they need to understand the sources themselves. They should know what each source is, who created it, what kind of source it is, what perspective it offers, and how it contributes to the larger assignment.

This stage helps students read sources as meaningful texts rather than as quote banks. It prepares them to examine evidence with attention to context, purpose, audience, credibility, and significance.

### Evidence Collection

Students begin gathering observations, quotations, examples, and details that seem important. At this stage, the emphasis is on noticing and recording evidence accurately, not on forcing that evidence into a complete argument too early.

The goal is to help students build a usable body of material. Students should be encouraged to collect enough evidence to compare, revisit, and sort later. Evidence collection should feel like building a library of possibilities.

### Pattern Discovery

After enough evidence has been gathered, students begin looking across that evidence for repetition, contrast, tension, development, cause and effect, structure, strategy, or significance. This is where isolated notes begin to turn into thinking.

The goal is to help students move from "I found something" to "I see something happening." Pattern discovery is the bridge between collection and interpretation.

### Claim Formation

Students then begin forming claims based on the patterns they have discovered. A claim should emerge from evidence, not from guesswork or imitation. At this stage, students should be encouraged to test whether their claim is specific, defensible, and actually supported by what they have collected.

The goal is not to force a polished thesis immediately. The goal is to help students shape an idea that can guide further thinking and later become stronger through additional support.

### Evidence Expansion

Once a student has a claim, the work is not finished. This stage asks: What more do we need? What evidence is missing? Where is support thin? What counterexample, second example, comparison, or extension would make the claim more credible?

This stage is essential because real argument building is recursive. Students often need to go back for stronger, broader, or more precise support. Evidence expansion teaches that a claim becomes stronger when it is tested and fed with more evidence.

### Organization

Only after students have both a working claim and a body of support should they organize their writing. Organization means deciding how ideas should be grouped, sequenced, and developed so the writing communicates thought clearly.

The goal is to help students understand structure as a meaning-making tool. Organization is not just formatting. It is the act of arranging thinking so that readers can follow the logic of the argument.

### Drafting

Drafting turns structured thinking into written language. Students use their claim, evidence, and plan to create actual sentences, paragraphs, and sections of an essay or written response.

The goal is not perfect prose on the first try. The goal is to externalize thinking in a coherent form that can later be examined and improved.

### Revision

Revision is where students strengthen meaning, reasoning, support, clarity, and precision. They look again at whether their evidence truly supports their claim, whether their explanation is deep enough, whether their structure works, and whether their writing says exactly what they mean.

The goal is to teach revision as rethinking, not just correcting. Students learn that better writing comes from strengthening ideas, not merely editing sentences.

### Publication

Publication is the final stage where the student prepares work for submission, sharing, or presentation. At this point, the writing should represent the best version of the student's thinking.

The goal is to give closure and authenticity to the process. Publication matters because it signals that the student's thinking has become public, purposeful, and worth sharing.

## Evidence Library

The Evidence Library is the central instructional concept that anchors the entire writing process. Instead of treating evidence as something tied to a single module or single step, the engine should treat evidence as a growing, reusable body of support that belongs to the student's assignment-level thinking.

Students should begin by building a **minimum evidence library**. This means they collect enough evidence early on to begin noticing patterns and developing ideas, even if that library is still incomplete. The goal is not total coverage before moving forward. The goal is to build enough evidence to begin thinking productively.

Once collected, evidence should remain available throughout the assignment. Students should be able to return to earlier evidence during claim formation, organization, drafting, and revision. Evidence should not disappear simply because the student has progressed to a later stage.

Evidence should be reusable. A strong piece of evidence may support more than one emerging idea. Students should be able to compare, regroup, and repurpose evidence as their understanding develops. This reinforces the idea that evidence serves thought, not task completion.

Evidence should also be expandable at any time. If a student's argument becomes more ambitious, more nuanced, or more specific, the student should be able to return to the sources and add new evidence without feeling that they are "going backward." The engine should make expansion feel normal and intellectually responsible.

To make the library usable, evidence should be tagged and filtered. Tags may reflect source, topic, concept, strategy, theme, issue, perspective, or other meaningful categories. Filtering helps students focus on the evidence most relevant to the idea they are currently trying to build.

Most importantly, evidence should support **claims rather than modules**. The student should experience evidence as something gathered for the purpose of making sense, building support, and strengthening reasoning. The organizational structure of the platform should never overshadow that deeper instructional purpose.

## Guided Spiral

The Writing Learning Engine V2 should use a guided spiral rather than a purely one-way sequence.

Observe

↓

Notice

↓

Develop an idea

↓

Need more evidence

↓

Collect more evidence

↓

Strengthen idea

↓

Repeat until argument is supported.

This spiral reflects how real thinking works. Students rarely move from first observation to final argument in a straight line. More often, they notice something interesting, begin forming an idea, realize the support is too thin, return to the evidence, discover something new, and then revise or strengthen the idea.

This is better than a strictly linear workflow because it makes room for intellectual development. A linear model can make students feel that once they leave a stage, they are not supposed to return. That can produce shallow claims, weak support, and writing that sounds finished before the thinking is finished.

The guided spiral teaches students that returning to the evidence is a strength, not a mistake. It gives them permission to revise their thinking as they learn more. It also creates a more authentic writing process in which ideas become stronger through repeated contact with evidence.

Instructionally, the spiral also supports differentiation. Some students may need only one return to the evidence before a claim becomes solid. Others may need several rounds of collecting, comparing, and refining. The spiral allows the engine to support both without treating either path as incorrect.

## AI Coach

In this model, the AI functions as a coach for thinking.

The AI never tells students what to think. It does not hand students a claim, interpretation, paragraph, or completed answer. The student's ideas must remain the student's own.

Instead, the AI asks questions that push the student back into reasoning. It can ask what the evidence shows, why a detail matters, how two examples connect, what difference the source context makes, or whether the student has enough support to justify a conclusion.

The AI should be able to identify weak support. If a student is making a broad claim from a single example, using vague reasoning, or relying on evidence that does not fully match the claim, the AI should surface that weakness clearly and constructively.

When support is weak, the AI should recommend gathering more evidence. It should direct the student back toward the sources and help the student see what kind of evidence is missing: another example, a contrasting case, a more precise quotation, a stronger explanation, or a clearer connection.

The AI should also encourage deeper reasoning. It can prompt students to explain significance, analyze cause and effect, compare perspectives, refine language, or account for complexity. The aim is not to produce longer writing automatically. The aim is to produce better thinking.

Finally, the AI should prepare students for the next thinking task. At each stage, it should help students understand what comes next and why. If a student has gathered enough evidence, the AI can help them begin noticing patterns. If a student has a claim, the AI can help them test support. If a student has a plan, the AI can help them draft from it. In this way, the AI serves as a bridge from one cognitive task to the next.

## Evidence Strength

The Writing Learning Engine V2 should include a conceptual evidence-strength system that helps students understand the quality of support they currently have.

### Weak

Weak evidence support means the student has too little support, unclear support, poorly matched support, or evidence that has not yet been explained well enough to justify the idea being made.

This does not mean the student is failing. It means the idea is still under-supported and needs more work.

### Developing

Developing evidence support means the student has a meaningful start. The evidence connects to the idea, but the support may still be limited in range, depth, precision, or explanation.

At this level, the student is moving in a productive direction but still benefits from expanding or sharpening the evidence base.

### Strong

Strong evidence support means the student has enough relevant, well-matched, and well-explained evidence to support the idea with confidence. The claim is grounded, the reasoning is clearer, and the support has enough substance to carry into organization and drafting.

This evidence-strength system is **instructional guidance, not grading**. Its purpose is to help students understand whether an idea is ready to move forward or whether it still needs additional support. It should promote better thinking decisions, not act as a score.

## Student Experience

The student experience should feel like intellectual work that leads somewhere meaningful.

Students should feel **curiosity** because they are being invited to notice, question, compare, and discover rather than simply comply. The process should make room for genuine inquiry.

Students should feel **discovery** because their ideas are expected to emerge from interaction with evidence. They should experience the satisfaction of seeing an interpretation take shape through their own thinking.

Students should feel **confidence** because the engine gives them enough structure to succeed without taking over the thinking for them. Confidence should come from having support, not from being given answers.

Students should feel **ownership** because the final writing is recognizably theirs. Their evidence choices, patterns, claims, organization, and language should reflect their own cognitive work.

The overall experience should not feel like worksheet completion. It should feel like building understanding and turning that understanding into writing.

## Teacher Experience

For teachers, the engine should create visibility into student thinking, not just visibility into final products.

Teachers should be able to see **how students are thinking**, not only what they finally submitted. This includes the evidence students collected, the ideas they explored, the claims they tested, and the places where support was weak or strengthened over time.

Teachers should have access to **evidence history** so they can understand what students noticed, which sources students used, what evidence students returned to, and how the body of support developed across the assignment.

Teachers should also have access to **remediation history**. If a student struggled with support, returned for more evidence, revised a weak idea, or responded to coaching prompts, that history should be visible as part of the student's learning process.

The engine should make **growth over time** easier to see. Teachers should be able to notice whether a student is moving from surface observation to deeper analysis, from thin claims to supported claims, and from loosely assembled writing to stronger argument.

Finally, teachers should be able to **intervene when needed**. The engine should help them identify when a student is stuck, under-supported, overly dependent on weak evidence, or ready for more challenge. The system should support timely instructional action, not just retrospective grading.

## Future Vision

The Writing Learning Engine V2 is designed to support any Grade 6-12 essay assignment because it is built around universal cognitive moves rather than a single genre, text set, or workflow script.

Students in different grades and assignments may work with different sources, purposes, genres, and expectations, but the core progression remains usable across contexts: understand the task, build knowledge, understand sources, collect evidence, discover patterns, form claims, strengthen support, organize, draft, revise, and publish.

This architecture can support literary analysis, rhetorical analysis, explanatory writing, argument writing, synthesis, research-based writing, historical analysis, and other secondary writing tasks because all of them depend on the relationship between evidence, thinking, and communication.

What changes from assignment to assignment is not the instructional engine itself, but the content inside it: the kinds of sources students use, the kinds of patterns they are asked to notice, the kinds of claims they build, and the kinds of final products they produce.

The long-term vision is a writing system in which every assignment teaches the same deeper lesson: strong writing comes from strong thinking. When students learn to gather evidence carefully, test ideas honestly, strengthen support deliberately, and revise meaningfully, they can apply that process across subjects, genres, and grade levels.

This is the core promise of the Writing Learning Engine V2: not just helping students finish assignments, but helping them become more capable thinkers and writers over time.
