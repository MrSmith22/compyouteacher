# Module 3 V2 Design

## Purpose

Module 3 V2 is the transition point between **evidence collection** and **claim formation**.

In this version of the Writing Processor, students do not leave evidence behind and jump directly into thesis writing. Instead, they carry their Evidence Library forward, notice patterns inside it, test one interesting idea, evaluate whether their support is strong enough, optionally gather more evidence, and only then develop a claim and turn that claim into a thesis.

This module is designed around the principles of the Writing Learning Engine V2:

- evidence comes before claims
- claims are provisional before they are supported
- students construct meaning from evidence
- AI coaches thinking rather than supplying ideas
- writing is the product of a sequence of thinking tasks

## Module Progression

Review Evidence Library

↓

Notice Patterns

↓

Explore One Interesting Idea

↓

Connect Evidence To That Idea

↓

Evaluate Evidence Strength

↓

(Optional) Gather More Evidence

↓

Develop Claim

↓

Turn Claim Into Thesis

## Design Principles for Module 3 V2

Module 3 should feel like the moment when students begin to say, "I think I see something here," and then are taught how to test whether that idea can become an argument.

The module should therefore:

- reduce repetitive prompt answering
- keep evidence visible at all times
- focus students on one promising line of thought at a time
- normalize returning to the evidence when support is thin
- distinguish between an interesting idea, a working claim, and a thesis
- produce artifacts that make Module 4 organization easier and more meaningful

## Screen 1. Review Your Evidence Library

### 1. Screen title

Review Your Evidence Library

### 2. Cognitive goal

The student should re-enter their collected evidence as a body of thinking material, not as completed work. The main thinking move is: **What do I have so far, and which pieces seem most worth revisiting?**

### 3. Why this step comes now

This step comes first because claim formation should begin from evidence already gathered. Before students can notice patterns or develop ideas, they need to see the evidence library as a usable set of resources.

### 4. What the student sees

The student sees:

- the assignment question or central writing task
- the full Evidence Library gathered in earlier modules
- evidence cards or entries grouped by source
- tags or labels already attached to evidence
- previously saved observation notes
- a short explanation that this module is about moving from evidence toward a claim

### 5. What the student can interact with

The student can:

- sort or filter the Evidence Library
- open evidence by source, tag, or topic
- mark a small working set of evidence for closer review
- flag evidence that feels especially important, surprising, or repeated

### 6. What data is reused from previous modules

This screen reuses:

- the assignment focus
- source understanding from earlier work
- the full Evidence Library
- student observations
- evidence tags
- any earlier notes about significance, audience, purpose, context, or strategy

### 7. What new data is created

This screen creates:

- a **working evidence set** for Module 3
- student markers showing which evidence feels most important right now

### 8. What determines whether the student can continue

The student can continue when they have selected a focused working set of evidence large enough to compare meaningfully. The expectation is not perfection. The expectation is that the student has enough evidence in view to begin noticing relationships.

### 9. What coaching messages appear

Possible coaching messages:

- "You are not choosing your final evidence yet. You are choosing the evidence you want to think with."
- "Look for evidence that seems to repeat, contrast, complicate, or raise a question."
- "Choose enough evidence to compare, not just one favorite example."

### 10. What artifacts become available to later modules

This screen makes available:

- the student's working evidence set
- priority markers showing which evidence the student considers most promising

## Screen 2. Notice Patterns Worth Following

### 1. Screen title

Notice Patterns Worth Following

### 2. Cognitive goal

The student should move from isolated evidence to pattern recognition. The main thinking move is: **What seems to repeat, contrast, develop, or matter across these pieces of evidence?**

### 3. Why this step comes now

After reviewing evidence, the student is ready to look across it instead of at it one piece at a time. Pattern discovery must come before claim formation, because claims should emerge from relationships in the evidence rather than from unsupported opinion.

### 4. What the student sees

The student sees:

- the selected working evidence set from Screen 1
- evidence arranged for comparison
- prompts that direct attention to repetition, contrast, tension, change, or significance
- examples of what counts as a pattern without giving students an answer

### 5. What the student can interact with

The student can:

- compare evidence side by side
- group evidence that seems connected
- record two or more pattern notices
- choose one pattern that feels worth exploring further

### 6. What data is reused from previous modules

This screen reuses:

- the working evidence set
- source information
- observation notes
- evidence tags and filters

### 7. What new data is created

This screen creates:

- a set of **pattern notices**
- one **selected pattern** to pursue

### 8. What determines whether the student can continue

The student can continue when they have recorded more than one pattern notice and selected one pattern to investigate. The goal is to make a choice about where to think more deeply.

### 9. What coaching messages appear

Possible coaching messages:

- "A pattern is something happening across evidence, not just a topic word."
- "If two pieces of evidence seem to speak to the same idea, place them together and ask what they reveal."
- "If you notice a contrast, ask why that difference matters."

### 10. What artifacts become available to later modules

This screen makes available:

- the student's pattern notices
- the selected pattern that becomes the entry point for claim development

## Screen 3. Explore One Interesting Idea

### 1. Screen title

Explore One Interesting Idea

### 2. Cognitive goal

The student should begin moving from pattern to interpretation. The main thinking move is: **If this pattern matters, what idea might it be pointing toward?**

### 3. Why this step comes now

Once a student notices a pattern, the next step is not to jump straight to a thesis. The student first needs space to explore one possible meaning and test whether it is interesting enough to pursue.

### 4. What the student sees

The student sees:

- the selected pattern from Screen 2
- the evidence that contributed to that pattern
- prompts that distinguish summary from interpretation
- language that frames this as a provisional idea, not a final claim

### 5. What the student can interact with

The student can:

- write a short exploratory idea statement
- explain why that idea seems interesting or important
- revise the wording until it reflects what they actually mean

### 6. What data is reused from previous modules

This screen reuses:

- the selected pattern
- the supporting evidence linked to that pattern
- the student's earlier observation notes

### 7. What new data is created

This screen creates:

- an **exploratory idea**
- a short note about **why the idea matters**

### 8. What determines whether the student can continue

The student can continue when the idea does more than restate the topic or summarize the evidence. It must point toward an interpretation that can be tested with support.

### 9. What coaching messages appear

Possible coaching messages:

- "Do not try to sound finished yet. Try to sound accurate."
- "An interesting idea explains what the pattern might mean, not just what the evidence says."
- "If your idea could apply to almost anything, make it more specific."

### 10. What artifacts become available to later modules

This screen makes available:

- the student's exploratory idea
- the significance note attached to that idea

## Screen 4. Connect Evidence to the Idea

### 1. Screen title

Connect Evidence to the Idea

### 2. Cognitive goal

The student should test whether the idea is genuinely supported. The main thinking move is: **How does this evidence help support, complicate, or sharpen this idea?**

### 3. Why this step comes now

An interesting idea is not enough by itself. Before students judge evidence strength or develop a claim, they need to practice linking evidence to the idea in a deliberate way.

### 4. What the student sees

The student sees:

- the exploratory idea at the top of the screen
- the working evidence set beside or below it
- a workspace for matching evidence to the idea
- prompts that ask what each piece of evidence shows and why it matters

### 5. What the student can interact with

The student can:

- choose the strongest evidence that fits the idea
- map evidence directly to the idea
- add a short explanation of how each chosen piece supports, complicates, or sharpens the idea
- remove evidence that does not really fit

### 6. What data is reused from previous modules

This screen reuses:

- the working evidence set
- the selected pattern
- the exploratory idea
- original observation notes

### 7. What new data is created

This screen creates:

- an **evidence-to-idea map**
- brief **connection notes** showing why each selected piece matters

### 8. What determines whether the student can continue

The student can continue when the idea is connected to enough evidence to evaluate support meaningfully. The standard is not just quantity. The student must be able to explain why the chosen evidence actually fits.

### 9. What coaching messages appear

Possible coaching messages:

- "Evidence does not support an idea automatically. You have to explain the connection."
- "If you cannot explain why a piece fits, it may not belong in this line of thinking."
- "Choose the evidence that does the most work, not the evidence that simply looks familiar."

### 10. What artifacts become available to later modules

This screen makes available:

- the evidence-to-idea map
- the set of strongest supporting evidence for the chosen idea

## Screen 5. Evaluate Evidence Strength

### 1. Screen title

Evaluate Evidence Strength

### 2. Cognitive goal

The student should judge the quality of support before making a formal claim. The main thinking move is: **Is my support weak, developing, or strong, and what makes it so?**

### 3. Why this step comes now

This step comes after evidence has been connected to an idea because students can only judge support quality once they have tried to make the connection explicit. It prevents students from mistaking one promising idea for a well-supported argument.

### 4. What the student sees

The student sees:

- the exploratory idea
- the evidence-to-idea map
- a simple evidence-strength scale:
  - Weak
  - Developing
  - Strong
- prompts that ask students to consider relevance, range, and explanation

### 5. What the student can interact with

The student can:

- review the current support set
- choose an evidence-strength level
- identify what is currently weakest in the support
- decide whether they are ready to move forward or need more evidence

### 6. What data is reused from previous modules

This screen reuses:

- the exploratory idea
- the chosen supporting evidence
- the evidence-to-idea map
- earlier evidence annotations

### 7. What new data is created

This screen creates:

- an **evidence-strength assessment**
- a **gap note** explaining what support is missing or still thin
- a **path decision**:
  - move forward
  - gather more evidence

### 8. What determines whether the student can continue

If the support is judged strong enough, the student can continue to claim development.

If the support is judged weak or still developing in an important way, the student moves to the optional evidence-gathering screen before continuing.

### 9. What coaching messages appear

Possible coaching messages:

- "One interesting example is not always enough to support a larger idea."
- "Ask whether your evidence is relevant, varied enough, and well explained."
- "If the idea is strong but the support is thin, do not abandon the idea. Strengthen the support."

### 10. What artifacts become available to later modules

This screen makes available:

- the evidence-strength assessment
- the student's gap note

## Screen 6. Gather More Evidence

### 1. Screen title

Gather More Evidence

### 2. Cognitive goal

The student should return to evidence strategically rather than randomly. The main thinking move is: **What kind of evidence is missing, and what new evidence would make this idea more supportable?**

### 3. Why this step comes now

This step appears only when the student's support needs strengthening. It comes after evidence-strength evaluation because students first need to understand the weakness before they can gather better evidence.

### 4. What the student sees

The student sees:

- the exploratory idea
- the current evidence-strength assessment
- the gap note from Screen 5
- the full Evidence Library again
- prompts that guide the student toward targeted evidence collection

### 5. What the student can interact with

The student can:

- return to the Evidence Library
- add new evidence to the working set
- replace weak evidence with stronger evidence
- attach a short note explaining how each new piece strengthens the idea

### 6. What data is reused from previous modules

This screen reuses:

- the full Evidence Library
- the working evidence set
- the selected pattern
- the exploratory idea
- the evidence-strength assessment
- the gap note

### 7. What new data is created

This screen creates:

- an **expanded evidence set**
- **evidence-strengthening notes** explaining how new evidence helps
- a revised support set ready for claim development

### 8. What determines whether the student can continue

The student can continue when they have addressed the identified gap well enough to support the idea more credibly. The expectation is not endless collection. The expectation is purposeful strengthening.

### 9. What coaching messages appear

Possible coaching messages:

- "Do not gather more of the same if the problem is missing range."
- "Look for evidence that deepens, sharpens, or complicates your idea."
- "New evidence should answer the weakness you identified, not just increase the pile."

### 10. What artifacts become available to later modules

This screen makes available:

- the expanded evidence set
- the evidence-strengthening notes

## Screen 7. Develop a Working Claim

### 1. Screen title

Develop a Working Claim

### 2. Cognitive goal

The student should transform a supported idea into a defensible claim. The main thinking move is: **Given this evidence, what can I now argue with confidence?**

### 3. Why this step comes now

Claim development comes only after students have explored an idea and tested its support. This sequencing protects against premature thesis writing and helps students see claims as earned through evidence.

### 4. What the student sees

The student sees:

- the exploratory idea
- the strongest supporting evidence
- the evidence-strength judgment
- prompts that distinguish a supported claim from a loose thought or broad topic

### 5. What the student can interact with

The student can:

- write a working claim
- revise the claim for precision
- explain briefly why the evidence supports the claim
- narrow or sharpen the claim if it overreaches

### 6. What data is reused from previous modules

This screen reuses:

- the exploratory idea
- the evidence-to-idea map
- the strongest evidence set
- the evidence-strength assessment

### 7. What new data is created

This screen creates:

- a **working claim**
- a short **support rationale** showing why the claim is defensible

### 8. What determines whether the student can continue

The student can continue when the claim is specific, arguable, and clearly supported by the evidence in view. A claim that is too broad, too vague, or not yet supportable should be revised before moving on.

### 9. What coaching messages appear

Possible coaching messages:

- "A claim should say something you can support, not something anyone could say."
- "If your claim sounds like a topic label, make it more precise."
- "If the claim reaches beyond your evidence, narrow the claim rather than forcing the evidence."

### 10. What artifacts become available to later modules

This screen makes available:

- the working claim
- the support rationale attached to that claim

## Screen 8. Turn the Claim into a Thesis

### 1. Screen title

Turn the Claim into a Thesis

### 2. Cognitive goal

The student should convert a supported working claim into a thesis that can guide later organization and drafting. The main thinking move is: **How do I express this claim clearly as the central argument of my writing?**

### 3. Why this step comes now

The thesis comes last because it should be the written form of supported thinking, not the starting point. By this stage, the student has already noticed patterns, explored an idea, tested support, and developed a claim.

### 4. What the student sees

The student sees:

- the working claim
- the strongest supporting evidence or evidence clusters
- prompts that explain the difference between a claim and a thesis
- reminders that the thesis should reflect the student's own thinking
- guidance about how the thesis will lead into the next module

### 5. What the student can interact with

The student can:

- draft a thesis statement
- revise the thesis for clarity and precision
- identify the main lines of proof the thesis will need to develop later

### 6. What data is reused from previous modules

This screen reuses:

- the working claim
- the support rationale
- the strongest evidence clusters
- the selected pattern
- the evidence-strength judgment

### 7. What new data is created

This screen creates:

- a **thesis statement**
- a **proof plan** showing the main directions the thesis will need to prove in the next module

### 8. What determines whether the student can continue

The student can continue when the thesis clearly reflects the developed claim and the student has identified the main directions of support that will later shape organization.

### 9. What coaching messages appear

Possible coaching messages:

- "A thesis is not a new idea. It is your supported claim stated clearly."
- "Say what you can actually prove from your evidence."
- "Your thesis should point forward to the structure of your argument."

### 10. What artifacts become available to later modules

This screen makes available:

- the student's thesis statement
- the proof plan that will guide organization in the next module
- the chain of reasoning behind the thesis:
  - selected pattern
  - exploratory idea
  - evidence-to-idea map
  - evidence-strength judgment
  - working claim

## Module 3 V2 Artifacts Summary

By the end of Module 3 V2, later modules should be able to draw on:

- the working evidence set
- pattern notices
- the selected pattern
- the exploratory idea
- the evidence-to-idea map
- the evidence-strength assessment
- any evidence-expansion notes
- the working claim
- the thesis statement
- the proof plan

This gives later modules access not only to the final thesis, but also to the reasoning path that produced it.

## Why Module 3 V2 Is Instructionally Different

Module 3 V2 is designed to slow down the moment when students move from collected evidence to arguable writing. Instead of asking students to answer many parallel prompts and then produce a thesis, it teaches them how to:

- return to their evidence library with purpose
- recognize patterns before writing claims
- test an idea with actual support
- diagnose weak support without penalty
- gather more evidence when needed
- build a claim that has been earned
- turn that claim into a thesis that can guide later organization

This makes Module 3 the first true claim-building module in the larger writing progression.

## Comparison Table

| Dimension | Current Module 3 | Module 3 V2 |
|---|---|---|
| Number of screens | 11 total screens, including success; 10 instructional screens before completion | 8 core instructional screens, with 1 optional evidence-return screen |
| Number of student inputs | 18 discrete inputs: 4 audience/purpose responses, 12 appeal explanations, 1 organization choice, 1 thesis | About 9 core outputs: working evidence set, pattern notices, exploratory idea, evidence-to-idea map, evidence-strength check, working claim, thesis, and proof plan; plus optional evidence-expansion work |
| Cognitive load | High and distributed across many parallel prompts; students are asked to think about audience, purpose, appeals, comparison, and thesis in rapid succession | More focused and staged; students concentrate on one evidence-driven line of thinking at a time before moving toward a claim |
| Repeated work eliminated | Repeated restating of audience and purpose; repeated appeal explanations across both texts; repeated pressure to justify ideas before checking support strength | Eliminates repetitive prompt cycles by using one evidence library, one pattern path, one supported idea, and one claim-development sequence |
| New instructional benefits | Thesis emerges after substantial prompt completion, but support quality is not separately diagnosed and evidence return is not built into the process | Adds explicit pattern discovery, idea testing, evidence-strength evaluation, optional return for more evidence, stronger claim formation, clearer ownership of ideas, and a better handoff to later organization work |
